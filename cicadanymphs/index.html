No. 011 / Misunderstood Chinese Food

Zhiliao Hou: seventeen years underground, three minutes in the oil

An insect that spent up to seventeen years buried in soil, accumulating protein and fat with single-minded patience, emerges for one summer night — and becomes one of the most texturally extraordinary snacks in Chinese street food culture. This is not a dare. This is biology doing the prep work so the cook doesn't have to.

Not a dare

This is not a stunt or a fear-factor challenge. It is a seasonal delicacy with legitimate culinary and nutritional credentials.

Not rubbery or bland

Properly fried at 180–190°C, the exoskeleton becomes genuinely crispy — it shatters rather than bends — while the interior sets into a rich, creamy paste.

Not available year-round

The harvest window is brutally short: three to four weeks in late June to early August. Timing is everything.

What Is It?

An underground protein delivery system, now a summer tradition.

Fried Cicada Nymphs, known in China as Zhiliao Hou (知了猴) or simply Cicada Nymphs, are a traditional seasonal delicacy enjoyed across northern and eastern China. These insects are collected shortly before they emerge from the ground and transform into adult cicadas — while still soft-shelled, plump, and packed with the protein and fat reserves accumulated over years of feeding on tree root sap.

Unlike mature cicadas with wings, the nymph at this stage has a smooth shell and a mild flavor that becomes wonderfully crispy when deep-fried. Garnished with sea salt, cumin, chili powder, garlic, and green onions, it is a snack that rewards the patient — both the insect and the person waiting for cicada season every year.

The Anatomy

The abdomen is the whole argument.

The cross-section of a properly fried cicada nymph makes the case by itself: a crispy, golden-brown exoskeleton that shatters cleanly when bitten, surrounding a dense, rich interior with a protein density higher than beef. The contrast between the aggressive crunch of the shell and the soft, creamy, umami-packed center is not achieved by human technique — it is the result of seventeen years of biological preparation.

Myth vs Fact

The appearance is louder than the flavor.

Myth

It's just a dare or stunt food.

The internet loves to frame edible insects as challenges. In Shandong, families have been collecting nymphs for generations as a seasonal tradition.

Fact

60–65% protein by dry weight.

Higher protein density than beef, chicken, or most conventional protein sources. Plus significant unsaturated fat and glutamate for umami.

Myth

It tastes like dirt or bugs.

First-timers are often surprised. The flavor is nutty, savory, rich, and slightly earthy — frequently compared to roasted nuts or crispy shrimp shells.

Fact

Three minutes at 180°C is the science.

Lower than 180°C: greasy and soft. Higher than 190°C: burnt shell, raw interior. The 180–190°C window is non-negotiable.

How to Eat It

Three moves. Bring beer.

01

Whole, While Hot

Pick one up with chopsticks or fingers while still hot and crispy. Bite through the shell — it shatters cleanly — and enjoy the contrast with the creamy interior.

02

With Ice-Cold Beer

The Shandong night market standard: cold beer against the seasoned, nutty richness of the nymph. Sip, bite, repeat. The combination is deeply satisfying.

03

As a Shared Plate

Served as a snack or appetizer alongside grilled skewers, stir-fried vegetables, or rice. The social context matters — this is summer tradition food, meant to be shared.
